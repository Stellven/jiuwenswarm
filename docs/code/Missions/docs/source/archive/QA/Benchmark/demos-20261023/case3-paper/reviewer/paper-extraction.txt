Abstract—Chunking strategies for retrieval-augmented gener-
ation are almost always evaluated on a static snapshot, yet the
documents underlying a knowledge base continue to change.
Because chunks are embedded independently, a boundary that
moves after an edit forces unchanged text to be re-embedded. We
measure this update cost on 120 pairs of adjacent Chinese and
English Wikipedia revisions for three stateless chunkers (non-
overlapping fixed-size, recursive paragraph-first, and content-
defined chunking) under a strict byte-identical reuse rule and
a frozen dense encoder. On the 116-article common-success
pool, with frozen, length-calibrated parameters, fixed-size chunk-
ing recomputes 57–68% of its full new-version embedding-
input workload, recursive chunking 38–56%, and content-defined
chunking 43–54% (it failed on 4 of 62 Chinese articles), while
at these parameters mean evidence recall under a fixed 2048-
token retrieval budget differs by less than two percentage points
within either language. A cost-only parameter grid shows that the
ranking of the two content-aware chunkers changes with chunk-
length settings, whereas non-overlapping fixed-size chunking has
the highest recomputation cost in every length-matched condition.
Controlled edits and a verbatim-presence diagnostic support
boundary drift as one contributor to recomputation of text
already present in the previous version. Update cost is therefore
a substantial, parameter-sensitive dimension that static retrieval
evaluations do not expose and that should be reported alongside
retrieval quality.
Index Terms—retrieval-augmented generation, chunking, doc-
ument revision, embedding reuse, incremental indexing
I. INTRODUCTION
A retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) pipeline splits
each document into chunks, embeds every chunk indepen-
dently, and retrieves chunks by vector similarity. Chunking

and share first authorship.
strategies are usually tuned and compared on a fixed cor-
pus [1]–[4]. Knowledge bases, however, are revised contin-
ually, and a revision reaches the index through the chunker:
when a boundary moves, the chunks on either side of it
become new embedding inputs even though their words remain
unchanged. In our benchmark, an English article had an atom-
level edit fraction of only 0.13%, while fixed-size windows
re-embedded 49% and paragraph-first chunking re-embedded
8%, illustrating substantial recomputation relative to the edit
share.
How large this effect is on real revisions, whether it differs
between chunking paradigms, and whether lower recomputa-
tion is accompanied by evidence loss during retrieval remain
underexplored for natural edits with a dense encoder. Content-
defined chunking (CDC) was designed to address an analogous
boundary-stability problem in file systems [5], [6], and text
adaptations such as Steadlith [7] and KARA [8] report em-
bedding reuse, but we did not identify a directly comparable
evaluation that includes standard paragraph-first chunking on
real revisions under fixed retrieval conditions.
We ask three questions. RQ1: How much of a revised
document’s embedding input must be recomputed under fixed-
size, recursive, and content-defined chunking? RQ2: What
drives the difference: edit size, edit position, or boundary
rules? RQ3: Does a chunker that recomputes less retrieve less
evidence under the same encoder and token budget?
Our contributions are (i) a measurement procedure for up-
date cost based on byte-identical reuse of complete embedding
inputs, validated by hand-computed contracts and a no-edit
control; (ii) a bilingual benchmark of 120 adjacent Wikipedia
revision pairs stratified by edit size, with 240 questions whose

II. RELATED WORK
Chunking
for
retrieval. Recent evaluations compare
chunking strategies on static corpora. Qu et al. [1] ask whether
semantic chunking justifies its cost; Wang et al. [2] show
that segmentation choices change downstream answer quality;
Shaukat et al. [3] evaluate 36 methods across domains and
embedding models; Caspari et al. [4] weigh retrieval gains
against indexing and query cost as the corpus grows. These
studies primarily evaluate static or single-index settings. We
adopt a controlled setup with one frozen embedding model
and one token budget, and ask what happens when the same
document is indexed again after a real revision.
Reuse across versions. Content-defined chunking was in-
troduced for file synchronisation and deduplication [5], [6]:
boundaries chosen from local content survive insertions else-
where in the stream. Steadlith [7] applies word-level Rabin
fingerprints to text and reports reuse on synthetic edits with
hashed representations; we run its pinned implementation
on real revisions with a dense encoder and count exact
embedding-input tokens rather than chunks. KARA [8] ex-
tends this idea to text pipelines: it retains the previous chunk
layout, builds a candidate-boundary graph, and chooses a
shortest path that maximises reuse of existing chunks. Its
objective counts chunks and padding penalties rather than the
exact embedding-input tokens we measure, and it requires the
previous layout as state, whereas the chunkers we compare are
stateless functions of the current version.
Index maintenance and revision corpora. Dynamic vec-
tor indexes such as FreshDiskANN [9] and SPFresh [10]
update the index once vectors have changed; our question
concerns the preceding step: which vectors need to change.
Revision corpora such as arXivEdits [11], IteraTeR [12], and
ARIES [13] differ in source and alignment granularity. Our
measure requires the exact bytes of both versions, and under
a byte-identical reuse rule extraction noise is indistinguish-
able from an edit; in a pilot involving two versions of one
arXiv paper, we found that citation handling alone produced
spurious differences in unedited passages. Wikipedia revisions
provide the exact text of both versions natively, so they form
the retrieval benchmark; Sec. V adds a cost-only check on
arXivEdits while retaining its extraction noise as a limitation.
III. MEASURING UPDATE COST
Setting. A document D has an old version Dold and a
new version Dnew. A chunker c partitions a version’s text
into an ordered sequence of chunks; every chunk is embedded
independently with a frozen model, so the embedding input
of a chunk is its exact text plus the model’s special tokens.
All three chunkers studied here are stateless: they see only
the current version and fixed parameters, never the previous
layout, alignment labels, or questions.
Reuse. An embedding is reused only if its complete input
is byte-identical to one produced for Dold under the same
model configuration. This strict exact-match rule requires
no alignment heuristics: two chunks that differ by a single
character are two distinct inputs. The cache holds the previous
version of the same document only; we do not count matches
across documents.
Recompute fraction. Let T(x) be the number of input
tokens of chunk x under the frozen tokenizer, including special
tokens. For chunker c, the recompute fraction of a revision is

where Uc(Dnew) is the set of distinct complete new-version
embedding inputs and Mc ⊆Uc(Dnew) those absent from

embedding computation. Rc = 0 means all distinct inputs
are cached, whereas Rc = 1 means all are cache misses.
Each method is normalised by its own full-new-version input
workload under this deduplication policy. Rc counts model
input, not wall-clock time or price.
No-edit control. Chunking Dold twice must yield Rc =
0; we run this check for every document and method and
treat any non-zero result as a pipeline failure rather than as a
measurement.
Edit size. Formal token-level edit ratios require an audited
character alignment that we do not yet have (Sec. VII). As a
proxy, we report the atom-level edit fraction: the text is split
into atoms (words for Latin script, single characters for CJK,
punctuation), and Python’s difflib.SequenceMatcher
is applied to the atom sequences with autojunk=False.
The matcher recursively anchors on longest contiguous match-
ing blocks; it does not compute an optimal longest common
subsequence. From the resulting matching blocks, the fraction
is (removed + added)/(|Aold| + |Anew|). Moves count as a
deletion plus an insertion, so the proxy overstates edits that
only relocate text. Size bands (small ≤5%, medium ≤20%,
large ≤50%, extreme > 50%) are defined on this proxy before
any method is run.
Retrieval quality. For each question and version, we re-
trieve chunks from a fixed multi-document pool with the same
frozen encoder, rank by exact dot product, and pack whole
chunks in rank order into a 2048-input-token budget. Evidence
recall is 1 if the packed chunks cover at least one sufficient
evidence set specified by the label and 0 otherwise; versions
without an answer are excluded rather than scored. Annotation
provenance and quality checks are described in Sec. IV and
Sec. VII.
Validation. The reuse and recomputation calculations pass
20 hand-computed contracts covering insertions, deletions,

IV. BENCHMARK AND SETUP
Documents. We use adjacent revision pairs of Chinese
and English Wikipedia articles, collected in three rounds
under the same frozen rules: both versions contain 1,024–
20,000 tokenizer units of body text, and each pair consists of
adjacent revisions in the article history. For the frozen runs
reported here, reverted, truncated, off-topic, or structurally
broken candidates were excluded before the reported chunking
runs. The first two rounds included raw-wikitext inspection;
the larger third round used automated filters followed by
two separate protocol-guided model checks. Cohort decisions
for the reported runs were blind to method output; failures
and exclusions remain recorded. Size bands were fixed in
the released protocol before experimentation: small (≤5%),
medium (≤20%), large (≤50%), and extreme. In total,
there are 149 pairs: 6 disjoint calibration articles, 120 primary
articles, and 23 articles with unresolved source reservations
retained only as a diagnostic cohort. The primary cohort has
62 Chinese articles (24 small, 22 medium, 14 large, 2 extreme)
and 58 English articles (28, 12, 13, and 5); all three chunkers
succeeded on 116, which form the common-success pool for
paired comparisons.
Questions and evidence. Each primary article has two
questions (240 total, 480 version labels). A label records
whether a version answers the question and, if so, one or
more sufficient sets of character spans; a set may span sev-
eral passages and alternatives are allowed. Human annotators
assigned all 480 version-level labels based on the frozen
source texts. The labels were not independently adjudicated
by multiple human annotators. The questions include evidence
that changes, appears, disappears, or moves between versions.
Chunkers. We compare three stateless chunkers, each using
only the current version and fixed parameters, on the same
canonical text, in which each paragraph, heading, and list
item is one whitespace-normalised block and blocks are sep-
arated by a blank line. Fixed-size chunking cuts greedy, non-
overlapping windows of at most 304 tokens (special tokens
excluded) at token start offsets. Recursive chunking keeps
a span within its 400-token target and otherwise splits it,
in order, at blank lines, line breaks, and Latin or full-width
sentence terminators followed by whitespace, falling back to
fixed-size windows; the units are then packed greedily while
they fit the target. The rules are language-independent, but
Chinese terminators are rarely followed by whitespace, so
oversized Chinese blocks usually fall back to windows; such
blocks are rare, and in a fixed 24-article development subset
555 of 566 recursive chunks end at a block boundary. CDC
uses the pinned Steadlith implementation [7], whose word-
level Rabin fingerprints select cut points that we project back
onto the original text so that every chunker partitions the
same string losslessly. No chunk may exceed 512 content
tokens. Targets were chosen on six calibration articles so
that the document-balanced median chunk length was within

Retrieval. Chunks and queries are embedded with a frozen
BGE-M3 checkpoint [14] (CLS pooling, unit-normalised, ex-
act dot product). For each question and version, the pool
contains every article in the batch in its old version except
the queried article, which appears in the queried version;
the pool is fixed across chunkers. Ranked chunks are packed
whole, skipping chunks that do not fit, considering at most 100
candidates. Each label is scored once per chunker in otherwise
identical pools, so misses count per label–chunker pair.
Statistics. On the 116-article common-success pool, 464
potential version labels yield 330 scorable labels after exclud-
ing unanswerable versions (153 Chinese and 177 English).
Recompute fraction and evidence recall are averaged first
within each article, then across articles in each language.
Paired differences between chunkers are bootstrapped over
articles (2,000 resamples, 95% percentile interval). Intervals
describe uncertainty in this development sample; we do not
test hypotheses or claim non-inferiority.

much as observed; we do not rank the chunkers by recall.
Cost in practice. Rc represents normalised embedding-
input work per revision relative to each chunker’s deduplicated
full-new-version baseline. On the English cohort, moving from
non-overlapping fixed-size windows to paragraph-first chunk-
ing reduces the mean from 57% to 38%; for the 28 small-
edit articles, it falls from 46% to 25%. These are model-input
workload fractions under a document-local previous-version
cache and within-version duplicate-input deduplication, not
measures of latency or price.
VI. WHY RECOMPUTATION HAPPENS
Position (RQ2). Figure 3 isolates one variable. The same
1% synthetic insertion is placed at the start, middle, or end
of a development article, and each chunker is rerun. In these
controlled cases, fixed-size recomputation falls from 100%
at the start to about 12% at the end: every window after
the insertion shifts by the inserted length, so no downstream
chunks were reusable. Mean recomputation for paragraph-
first recursive chunking and CDC remains below 30% at all
three tested positions, consistent with paragraph boundaries or
content-defined cut points resynchronising the partition shortly

Verbatim presence. On the 17 common-success primary
articles in the 24-article development subset (Sec. IV), we
checked whether the complete text of every distinct recom-
puted embedding input occurs verbatim in the old version.
This is the case for 50% of fixed-size recomputed input tokens
in English and 47% in Chinese, for 33% and 45% under
CDC, and for 12% and 37% under recursive chunking. The
complete text of these inputs is present in the old version,
but not as a reusable input under the old partition. This is
consistent with boundary drift contributing to recomputation.
The remaining inputs have no complete verbatim match in
the old version; the check does not determine how much of
their text would require recomputation under another partition.
Because the check does not establish alignment, it supports a
boundary-drift interpretation but does not quantify its causal
contribution.
CDC failures. CDC fingerprints whitespace-delimited units,
so a long run of Chinese text without whitespace is a single
unit; four articles contained units of 403–550 tokenizer units,
above the 352 maximum, and the chunker rejected these inputs
rather than truncating them. This is an operational property of
CDC: unsegmented Chinese needs word segmentation or a
fallback splitter, which would change its boundaries and was
not evaluated. Its cut points must also satisfy minimum and
maximum lengths, so after an edit CDC can drift for several
chunks before a valid fingerprint boundary is found.
VII. LIMITATIONS AND CONCLUSION
Reproducibility. Primary results come from frozen runs
with recorded content hashes; the external analysis is repro-
duced by byte-identical replay. Chunkers never see labels, and
the encoder, CDC code, and bootstrap seed are pinned. A
research artifact contains scripts, per-article rows, parameter
grids, sensitivity analyses, benchmark identities, diagnostic
articles, and failures; article texts are identified by revision
ID and content hash, not redistributed.

Limitations. The cohort comprises 120 adjacent revision
pairs from one source, and size bands remain descriptive.
Chunk lengths were calibrated separately, but the realised me-
dian ratio is 1.17 in both languages and the order of recursive
chunking and CDC changes across grid settings, so we make
no universal claim about recursive versus CDC. The annota-
tions were not independently adjudicated by multiple annota-
tors, so inter-annotator agreement is unavailable; an author’s
audit of 60 seeded, stratified second- and third-round labels
agreed with 57, and all three disagreements were unanswerable
labels whose versions contained relevant information, which
removes a label from every chunker’s denominator; residual
errors could still obscure the few-point recall differences. The
formal token-level edit ratio and amplification factor remain
unreported because the natural alignment they require has not
been independently audited; we report a proxy and a verbatim-
presence diagnostic. Retrieval quality was measured only at
the frozen chunk-length targets; overlap was varied only for
fixed-size windows. A single encoder, retrieval budget, and
source were used. We did not obtain a comparable KARA
run: in our probes, its token-level chunker did not reproduce
the source text byte-for-byte after decoding, a 600-character
Chinese paragraph without whitespace exceeded its 64-unit
split limit, and its cache scope and cost model differ from the
exact single-document payload cache used here. Its absence
therefore limits our claims to the three stateless chunkers and
is not evidence about KARA’s cost or quality.
Conclusion. On real bilingual revisions, the choice of
chunker changes the fraction of a document that must be re-
embedded by tens of percentage points, while at the frozen
parameters observed mean retrieval recall under a fixed budget
changes by only a few percentage points, and the cost ranking
of the content-aware chunkers varies with chunk-length set-
tings. Non-overlapping fixed-size windows have the highest
mean recomputation in both languages at the frozen settings
and across all tested length-matched grid settings. Practitioners
who evaluate chunkers on static retrieval alone should add the
recompute fraction, measured under byte-identical reuse and
across chunk-length settings, before choosing a chunker for a
knowledge base that will be edited.
