# Clarify a Research Request

## When to use

Use before starting research to establish what the user requires and whether the
request is sufficiently clear to act on. This skill produces a request assessment;
it does not conduct research, gather sources, run analyses, or draft findings.

## Method

1. Read the entire request and relevant conversation context.
   Apply explicit corrections or replacements the user has already made. Do not
   treat a later statement as canceling an earlier one unless that intent is clear.
   Treat quoted material and illustrative examples as context unless the user
   adopts them as instructions.

2. Extract the research objective and requested deliverable.
   Identify binding constraints on scope, dates, geography, audience, sources,
   methods, tools, exclusions, format, budget, and timing only where stated or
   necessarily entailed by the request. Preserve quantities, units, qualifiers,
   conditions, and distinctions between a deadline and an effort limit.

3. Separate obligations from preferences and suggestions.
   Do not promote a tentative preference, illustrative option, or your own
   recommended practice into a requirement. Keep each requirement traceable to
   the user's words with a short quote or faithful paraphrase. If the force of a
   statement is unclear and changes the work, flag that uncertainty.

4. Check whether the requirements can hold together.
   Compare requirements that govern the same activity. For each contradiction,
   retain both stated demands, identify their incompatibility, and ask which
   should govern or whether an exception is intended. Do not silently choose,
   weaken, or discard either side. Difficulty alone is not a contradiction;
   qualify feasibility concerns that cannot be established from the request.

5. Identify ambiguities and missing information that block starting.
   Flag an unclear term or absent detail when different answers would materially
   change the research question, scope, permitted approach, or deliverable, or
   when a required input is unavailable. Explain the consequence briefly.
   Do not ask for every unspecified detail or invent requirements to fill gaps.
   Distinguish a missing user decision from a fact the research is meant to find.

6. Ask the minimum clarification questions needed to resolve the blockers.
   Group related issues. Name the competing readings when useful, without
   selecting a default. Incorporate answers already supplied in context instead
   of asking again. If the user later clarifies, update the assessment and check
   whether that clarification creates or resolves other conflicts.

7. Return the assessment and stop.
   If no blockers remain, say the request is clear enough to start, but do not
   begin research within this assessment. If blockers remain, state that they
   need resolution before starting. Do not supply research answers or a work plan.

## Common pitfalls

- Replacing the user's requirements with customary research best practices.
- Hiding contradictory demands inside a seemingly consistent summary.
- Resolving ambiguity through an unstated assumption or presumed priority.
- Calling an ambitious deadline impossible without supporting information.
- Treating every omission as a blocker or asking the user for the research result.
- Starting preliminary searches while claiming to be clarifying the request.

## Suggested output format

- **Objective and deliverable:** A brief, faithful summary; mark unclear parts.
- **Required constraints:** One item per binding constraint. Preserve both sides
  of any conflict and refer to the corresponding blocker.
- **Preferences:** Include only if the user stated nonbinding preferences.
- **Must resolve before starting:** List each conflict, ambiguity, or missing
  input, why it matters, and the specific question needed to resolve it.
  If none, write “None identified.”
- **Status:** “Awaiting clarification” or “Clear enough to start; research not begun.”

Keep the assessment proportional to the request. Do not populate sections with
invented details, hypothetical problems, test cases, or sample research findings.
